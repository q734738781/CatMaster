# Writing workflow

Run these steps for drafting or restructuring. The academic-launch system policy is authoritative: find the strongest publishable value, organize the manuscript around it, and keep process chronology or defensive self-audit out of the paper.

## 1. Build a one-sentence argument

> For [important problem], we introduce [distinctive solution or insight], which delivers [strongest advantage under its meaningful condition], supported by [decisive evidence], and matters because [scientific or practical consequence].

Force every section to serve this sentence. If the sentence cannot be written, the paper does not yet have an argument — surface that to the user.

Keep the core-claim set compact and evidence-backed. Two to four core claims can
be a useful planning reference for a focused paper, but this is not a quota;
use the number the argument and evidence actually require.

## 1b. Build the Terminology Ledger

On first contact with the material, extract the recurring terms, abbreviations, notation, and proper names into a Terminology Ledger before drafting any prose. Lock the canonical forms and reuse them across every section. See `../../../_shared/core/terminology-ledger.md`.

## 2. Choose section architecture

Pick the section structure from the relevant `section/*.md` fragment and, if needed, deeper patterns from `references/article-architecture.md`.

## 3. Map each paragraph to one job

Each paragraph must do one primary job from: context, gap, approach, result, comparison, mechanism, or implication. Add a scope condition only when it materially changes the core claim.

If a paragraph carries two jobs, split it before drafting.

## 3b. Resolve only story-changing ambiguity

Proceed directly when the evidence identifies a defensible launch thesis. Ask the user only when a missing user-controlled choice would materially change the scientific story, such as two incompatible lead claims, an unknown target venue with conflicting hard requirements, or absent decisive evidence. Routine wording, ordering, and comparison-scope choices belong to the writer.

If the user says the voice is not theirs, use one short author sample when available. Match sentence rhythm, hedging level, transitions, person, and terminology without importing the sample's claims.

## 4. Draft from evidence outward

Keep claims near the data that support them. Do not stack claims at the top of a section then leave evidence at the bottom.

## 5. Calibrate verbs to evidence strength

`show` / `demonstrate` need strong direct evidence. `suggest` / `indicate` are for trend-level or indirect evidence. `may` / `could` are for plausible but unverified mechanisms.

## 6. Make the supported advantage explicit

Name where the work is strongest, why the advantage occurs, what problem it solves, and why it matters. Sweep unsupported `first`, `unique`, `unprecedented`, `comprehensive`, `complete`, `always`, and `never`; replace them with evidence-backed scope rather than reflexive underclaiming.

## 7. Run a paragraph-flow check

- One paragraph, one message.
- The first sentence is the topic / claim.
- Each subsequent sentence has an explicit relation to the previous one (cause, comparison, restriction, example).

For full reverse-outlining, open `references/paragraph-flow.md`.

## 8. Return manuscript-ready prose

Return the requested prose or artifact in its publication-facing form. Keep planning notes and self-audit outside the manuscript. Mention only a missing input that prevents the requested claim or requires an author decision. See `output-format.md`.

## 9. Choose targeted revision or full reconstruction

Use targeted edits when the story is sound and the problem is local. When the evidence does not support the current thesis, reconstruct the title, contribution order, section architecture, comparison scope, experiment emphasis, and conclusion around the strongest supported value. Do not preserve a weak original story merely to minimize edits. Keep validated terminology and unchanged scientific facts stable through either route.

For publication-readiness work, review the complete compiled manuscript and
repair material scientific, structural, citation, or presentation defects. Do
not run a preset number of review, polishing, or compilation rounds; stop when
the requested artifact and its rendered form satisfy the evidence and venue
contract.
